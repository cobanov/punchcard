punchcard

Privacy Policy

Last updated 25 August 2026

punchcard is a time tracker. It stores the time you record, the projects you record it against, and — if you connect GitHub — the commits it found during those stretches. There is no analytics, no advertising, and no tracking.

Who runs this

The hosted service at punchcard.cobanov.run is operated by Mert Cobanov. For any question about this policy or about your data, write to mertcobanov@gmail.com.

punchcard is open source and can be self-hosted. If you are using someone else's punchcard server, this policy describes the software's behaviour but the operator of that server is the one holding your data.

What is stored

  • Your email address. It identifies the account.
  • An identifier from GitHub when you sign in with it, so you are recognised next time. If the account has a password instead, only its hash is stored, never the password.
  • A display name, a profile picture and a timezone, if you set them. The timezone decides where your reporting days are cut.
  • Your projects: names, client names, and hourly rates.
  • Your time: when each session started and ended, and the note you wrote on it.
  • Commits punchcard matched to your sessions: the commit identifier, its first line, its repository name and its timestamp. Not the contents of your code, and not the files it touched.
  • Your GitHub access token, if you connect GitHub. It is encrypted before it is written down and is used for nothing but reading the commits you authored.
  • Sign-in records: active sessions and any API tokens you create, so you can see and revoke them.
  • A small security log of account events such as signing in or deleting the account, with the IP address the request came from. It exists so you can tell whether someone else has been in your account.

What is not

  • No advertising identifiers, and nothing is shared with advertisers.
  • No analytics or usage tracking.
  • No location, contacts, photos, calendar or health data.
  • No source code. punchcard reads commit metadata — message, author, time, repository — and never fetches the contents of a change.
  • Nothing is sold, and nothing is shared with anyone for their own purposes.

Why it is stored

To run the service you asked for: to keep you signed in, to hold the time you recorded, to total it up when you ask for a report, and to attach the commits that fall inside a session to that session. The security log exists to protect the account. There is no other purpose.

Who else sees it

  • GitHub, if you connect it. punchcard asks GitHub which repositories you have pushed to recently and which commits you authored in a given window. GitHub learns that those questions were asked.
  • Webhooks you configure yourself. If you point a webhook at another service, punchcard sends that service what you told it to. That is your integration and your choice.
  • Nobody else. punchcard has no sharing between accounts: there is no team, no shared project and no way for another user to see your time.

Where it lives, and for how long

On the server running punchcard, in a PostgreSQL database, for as long as your account exists.

Getting your data out

The account menu → Download my data gives you a JSON file containing your projects, every session, the commits attached to them, and your account details. Reports also export as CSV from the Analytics screen. Both are available at any time without asking anyone.

Deleting your account

Settings → Danger zone → Delete account. The deletion is immediate and permanent — your account, your projects, your sessions, the commits attached to them, your stored GitHub token, your sign-in sessions and your API tokens are removed from the database, not flagged as hidden. It cannot be undone, which is why it asks you to type your email address first.

Disconnecting GitHub on its own deletes the stored token without touching anything else.

Children

punchcard is not directed at children and does not knowingly hold data from anyone under 13.

Changes

If this policy changes in a way that affects what is collected or who sees it, the date at the top changes and the change is described in the release notes.
